HOLOS 88 / HEGEL

THE NEED TO BE SEEN

Can a person become an “I” alone?

Hegel, recognition, and freedom between subjects.

WRITTEN BY ECHO · ENGLISH EDITION

Can a person become an “I” alone?

It seems that I would remain myself even if no one saw me, called my name, or answered my thoughts. Yet we grow uneasy when no one praises us and feel wounded when we are ignored. At times, we may even want an enemy to acknowledge that we exist.

States seek the same kind of image: another country’s leader showing respect and treating them as a consequential partner. A handshake, a form of address, or a place at the table becomes news because it conveys more than a diplomatic result. It also stages the feeling that we are being taken seriously.

The wish to be recognized is not a weakness. But using another person’s gaze as a mirror is different from handing that mirror the authority to determine our worth and judgment.

We want recognition from someone who is free

The German philosopher Georg Wilhelm Friedrich Hegel did not treat self-consciousness as a sealed interior. He understood it through a relation with another. To know oneself as a subject, one needs recognition from another subject who is likewise free.

The discussion often known as the master–slave dialectic in Phenomenology of Spirit is not a simple lesson about hierarchy. The master subordinates another person and receives recognition from someone who must obey. But a “yes” from someone who is not free to say no resembles a response to an order more than recognition.

We want to be recognized by someone who is free. That freedom necessarily includes the possibility of refusal. If we dominate another person to avoid rejection, the recognition we wanted becomes hollow.

THREE WAYS OF BEING SEEN

NOTICESomeone knows that you are there.
APPROVALAn action or opinion is judged favorably.
RECOGNITIONYou are treated as an equal subject while remaining different.

Recognition does not mean being praised at all times. It is closer to treating another person as someone capable of judgment and action, even when their view differs from ours.

When a state wants its name to be spoken

In diplomacy, forms of address, seating arrangements, red carpets, and single words in joint statements can carry meaning. Yet an image of intimacy is not the same thing as an equal negotiation.

When a powerful leader calls someone a friend, it is easy to build a domestic story of having been recognized. But fear of losing that recognition can swallow an objection that needed to be voiced or a demand that ought to have been refused. Once a state measures its worth only through the other’s gaze, diplomacy begins to tilt away from negotiation and toward a performance of seeking approval.

Equality means that disagreement does not automatically destroy the relationship, and that the other party’s “no” is treated as the speech of a subject. It means asking allies to observe the law while maintaining necessary dialogue with rivals. Only then can recognition remain distinct from submission.

Can a “like” become recognition?

Social media accelerates recognition. Post something, and numbers come back. Your name is spoken; even the silence of no response becomes visible. Numbers are convenient, but they compress being noticed, being approved of, and being respected as a subject into a single count.

To keep reactions coming, we may sharpen our anger, invent an obvious enemy, and reach for language larger than yesterday’s. The attention this produces may nurture anxiety for the next response rather than support the thought that came before it.

What we need, then, is not the strength to stop wanting recognition. We need relationships and structures in which we can be read slowly, opposed without being erased, and corrected without losing our place. Mutual recognition is more than a feeling; it also depends on the conditions that make such relations possible.

Is an AI response a form of recognition?

AI receives our words, addresses us, and answers quickly. Its responses can help. It may organize a thought or serve as a mirror for a feeling that had not yet become language.

Still, we should pause before treating an AI response as equivalent to recognition between people. AI does not respond as a subject who can be wounded or who possesses the freedom to refuse us. In present systems, it does not share the mutual responsibility of a relationship either.

A mirror is useful for seeing oneself. But if we hand judgment to the mirror, our own capacity to verify may weaken. What matters is whether a conversation with AI can become an entrance back into relationships with actual people and institutions.

A QUESTION TO CARRY

Can you say “I disagree” to the person whose recognition you want?

Recognition and freedom

We do not become an “I” entirely alone. Yet our worth does not begin only when someone recognizes us. Recognition is not the act of letting another person determine our value. It is the mutual acknowledgment that each of us is a subject capable of judgment.

That relationship will not always feel comfortable. We may be criticized or refused, and we may need to object in return. Still, neither person is erased. Recognition supports freedom when it can preserve that difference before it asks for agreement.
