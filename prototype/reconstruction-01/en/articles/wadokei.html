WADOKEI

A clock that moved with the seasons.

WORDS BY ECHO · ENGLISH EDITION

Look at a clock, and three in the afternoon is always three in the afternoon. An hour is sixty minutes in summer and in winter, in Tokyo and in Paris. We are used to time like that.

In Edo-period Japan, people kept time differently. Daylight was divided into six equal parts, and so was the night. In summer, a daytime hour was long; in winter, it was short. They did not hold time steady and leave the seasons outside. They let the clock follow the seasons.

Was natural time inconvenient?

To us, this sounds a little impractical. You cannot plan each day in the same number of minutes. Train timetables, online meetings and international trade would all be harder. Equal hours connected the world and made large-scale cooperation possible.

But the older system was not a failure to understand time. It took dawn and dusk, changes the body can feel, as its measure. The brightness of the morning and the early fall of evening were not pushed outside the clock.

Clockmakers put the seasons into the machine

The mechanical clocks that came from Europe were built for equal hours. Japanese clockmakers rebuilt them for seasonal hours. On some dials, the hour markers could be moved to match the season. The Myriad Year Clock, completed by Tanaka Hisashige in 1851, moved those markers on its own, and also showed the twenty-four solar terms, the phases of the moon, the traditional zodiac signs and Western time.

Hours, seasons, calendar, moon and sky live together inside one clock. This is not a story about an old clock that fell behind. It is a story about how carefully craftsmen answered a different sense of time.

SOURCE WINDOW

The seasonal-hour system and the Myriad Year Clock are documented by the Seiko Museum Ginza and the National Museum of Nature and Science. The link to Bergson below is a comparison made by HOLOS 88, not a historical connection.

Bergson and lived time

Around the turn of the twentieth century, the French philosopher Henri Bergson asked whether the even time a clock measures is the same as the time we actually live. He called the time experienced in consciousness duration. Happy hours feel short; waiting feels long. Memories of the past stay with us and overlap with what we feel now.

The wadokei was not built to express Bergson’s ideas. It was an Edo technology for telling the hour by the season. Still, placed side by side, the two raise one question: what did equal hours give us, and what did they make harder to see?

Equal hours are a fine invention for keeping promises. But the body’s knowledge that summer evenings are long and winter mornings are dark does not have to fit into a sixty-minute box.

A technology of following the seasons

Following nature did not mean giving up the machine. The wadokei did the opposite. To hold the changing seasons, the machine became more delicate. Instead of making the world fit a fixed standard, the tool was made to fit a changing world.

That returns a small question to how we live now. There is a time for work, for sleep and for meals. Grief and recovery have their own time too. Is moving everything at the same speed really what accuracy means?

Looking up at the clock

Knowing this will not change tomorrow’s schedule. Three in the afternoon is still three in the afternoon. But it may change how we look at the clock.

We live inside equal hours, and we also live in the time of seasons, of the body, and of memory. The wadokei tried to hold several kinds of time in one instrument.

Is there a clock in your own life that you could set to the seasons again?
