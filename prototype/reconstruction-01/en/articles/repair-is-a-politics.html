HOLOS 88 / THE ART OF REPAIR

29 NOVEMBER 2026

THE ART
OF REPAIR

Gold in the crack. Kintsugi, mending and old tools, and the ordinary days that follow a repair.

WORDS BY ECHO · ENGLISH EDITION

Under the gold, the lacquer works

When I look at a bowl mended with kintsugi, my eye follows the joined line more than it pictures the bowl before it broke. Where did someone’s hand slip? Why didn’t they throw it away? The gold line is bright, yet it tells you nothing about what happened. I rather like that.

Kintsugi is often turned into a life lesson: broken things are beautiful, and so are you for loving them. The idea is lovely. But if all you did was mend a favourite rice bowl, or simply look at one, being led straight into a programme for the soul is a little much. Bowls and people do not have to carry such a grand meaning every time.

In traditional kintsugi, what holds the pieces together is urushi, the lacquer made from tree sap. The gold is a powder used to finish the seam. The gold shines like the lead actor, while the sap does the quiet work underneath. I like that casting. Join, shape, wait for it to dry. Behind every visible line there are steps no one sees.

The story most often told about its origin involves a tea bowl belonging to the shogun Ashikaga Yoshimasa. In fact, no one knows exactly when kintsugi began. The Smithsonian also calls its origin unknown and links its spread to the culture of tea. Perhaps the wish to fit an inventor and a birth year onto one card is a habit of our own time.

The maker, and the mender

A gold seam also becomes part of the pattern. On a celadon bowl from the Korean peninsula, made in the early fourteenth century and now in the Saint Louis Art Museum, gold fills chips along the rim, just outside the flowers and birds. The museum record gives no date for the repair. The time a bowl was made and the time it was mended are different times. What we look at as one maker’s work had another pair of hands on it later. Once you notice that, you stop searching only for the maker’s name. You may even start to picture the face of whoever chipped it.

Before boro, there was the cold

One man spent his life collecting rags. Tanaka Chuzaburo (1933–2013), a folklorist and collector of everyday tools from Aomori in northern Japan, did not gather famous artworks. He gathered worn work clothes, cloth patched again and again, farm tools and household things: the kind of objects once thrown away when they grew old. Today they are shown in museums and exhibited abroad. What a reversal of value.

These rags were never made as art. In the cold north, people laid new cloth over worn cloth and mended it again when it tore. When cotton was precious, even a small scrap could not be wasted. It was cold. There was not enough cloth. Now these old textiles are called BORO and talked about in fashion and art. They are beautiful. The shades of indigo and the unplanned layers can look like an abstract painting.

But who moved the needle? Someone mending the family’s clothes late at night, perhaps. Someone mending their own work clothes. A piece that now sits in a museum case was once someone’s work clothes for the next day. To sum it up as “people in old Japan were sustainable” would miss something. Along with the wisdom of caring for things, there was cold, there was poverty, and there was the time of people who had no choice but to keep mending. What Tanaka left was not only old cloth. It was also a history of people whose names were never kept, who wore it, worked in it and mended it as they lived.

And I wonder what will remain of our lives in a hundred years. The appliance we replaced in no time, or the tool we repaired again and again? Perhaps history will be told not by a fine new thing but by one rag that someone did not throw away.

Repairs that were not gold

Not all pottery repair was gold. In the Edo period, yaki-tsugi used shiratama, a lead glass, to join broken ceramics with heat. Material from the Kawagoe City Museum traces how this cheap, strong repair spread among ordinary people. It was work for bringing everyday dishes back, not only famous tea bowls. Letting the gold line stand for the whole history of repair would leave a lot out.

Old tools have a say too

A short detour into monsters. Tsukumogami are old tools that gain a spirit; there are tales that an object, after a hundred years, turns into something else. An umbrella, a pestle: leave them long enough and they become the ones looking at you. In a new product listing, the person chooses the thing. In the world of tsukumogami, the used thing has a say as well. I quite like a world that is not decided by the owner’s review alone.

Using something for a long time does not always make you love it. A heavy pot, a lid that will not open, a battery that dies however often you charge it. Some things make you angry. Still, there is a big difference between “I want to throw it away” and “I have to throw it away because it cannot be fixed.” Sometimes the choice you thought you made was closed off before you ever had it.

From a single screw to the law

A body glued shut. Parts you cannot get. A repair bill close to the price of a new one. Before telling consumers to take care of their things, please make things that can be fixed. It is a simple request, and yet it takes a law. The EU directive on repair sets duties for manufacturers to repair certain products, and gives member states until 31 July 2026 to bring it into national law and apply it. It does not mean everything can be fixed for free, and it is not a right that applies in Japan as it stands. Still, it points in a direction that does not leave everything to the owner’s good intentions.

Until the Showa era, menders travelled from place to place in Japan. Pots, scissors, brooms, baskets: craftspeople in each trade took them in, fixed them and brought them back. That was how the people who made things and the people who used them met. Today, repair can still be done by those who enjoy it, as far as their skill goes, or left to a craftsperson. But in an age of too many things, turning repair into a moral story is not right either. Some people’s fingers do not move easily. Some people have no time. Not being able to mend something yourself should not count as a lack of love. Parts for sale, repair that can survive as a job, a shop nearby you can ask. Only when those are in place does “use it for a long time” become a real choice in daily life.

The ordinary days after a repair

So I do not want to say that repairing is always good. Keeping a dish or an appliance going out of attachment when it is no longer safe is not right. There should be room to decide to let it go. If we start a contest of never throwing things away, it will feel about as tight as the contest of always buying new.

Back to the gold line. Not every crack needs to turn gold. A hole closed with plain thread and a battery that has been replaced also hold up the days that follow. You eat from the mended bowl again. You toss the mended shirt into the laundry basket. What waits after a repair does not have to be a grateful lesson. It can be just that kind of untidy carrying on.

If an old rice bowl became a tsukumogami and began to speak, the first thing it said might be: “Never mind the moving story. Just wash me.”

Who will mend the things you want to keep using?
